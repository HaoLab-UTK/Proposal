## What exactly does the call ask for, and how is it scored?

### Takeaway
The FY2026 104g General call is narrowly about observing, estimating, validating
and forecasting the water use of data centers and other water-intensive
industries that draw from public supply. AI/ML is a "central requirement", and
proposals are expected to validate against independent or ground-truth data and
to cover seasonal variability, water reuse and self-supply. The biggest scored
block (20 of 100 points) is alignment with Executive and Secretarial Orders,
including SO3444 on AI. A USGS letter of support is required.

### Inferences
- The scope centres on direct, facility-level water use from public supply, plus reuse and self-supply. Off-site thermoelectric (Scope 2) water is not named. An idea led by Scope 2 needs to be framed as a variable or driver of "water use associated with data centers", or it risks a weaker "address specific priority" score.
- $120K is a small award for a 3-year window. Roughly $40K/yr federal supports about one partial graduate assistantship plus a small PI summer share. Because federal funds cannot pay indirect costs, UT's unrecovered indirect costs on the federal direct costs can carry much of the 1:1 match. UT/TNWRRC must confirm this.
- With 4 awards nationally and 20 points for order alignment, the narrative must explicitly name SO3444 (AI), EO14303 (transparent, reproducible science, open code and data) and EO14239 (local preparedness for drought and heat).

### Gaps
- The TNWRRC internal deadline for 104g pre-review was not found. The TNWRRC homepage (https://tnwrrc.tennessee.edu/) shows no 104g notice. Contact the center directly.
- The NOFO's "Section III" research priorities and the full Executive Order text were not read beyond the summaries quoted in the NOFO.
- No public list of prior 104g awards on data center water was found. This is the first year with this topic (inferred from the NOFO wording), but that is unconfirmed.

## Who already works on this? (funded projects and literature, relevant to all five ideas)

### Takeaway
National-scale estimates (LBNL, Siddik et al. 2021) and the Shaolei Ren group's
national and site-level analyses are well established. USGS's own Water Mission
Area started an internal Scope 1 data center water-use project in 2026 that
explicitly excludes Scope 2 and uses NLP on literature and utility filings.
Minnesota Sea Grant funds a facility-level real-time estimation tool. Nothing
found does Tennessee- or TVA-specific, ML-based, validated Scope 1+2 estimation,
satellite or aerial detection of data center cooling, or coupling of
flash-drought and heatwave hydrology with siting-policy scenarios.

### Cited Findings
**USGS internal work (most important for Element 4 and for duplication risk)**
- An ORISE posting DOI-USGS-2026-33, "USGS Data Center Water Use" (Baltimore, MD; mentor Catherine Christenson; start 2026-06-15), states that the project "specifically addresses ... direct water use by data centers for cooling at the site level (Scope 1 use) but does not address indirect water use associated with data center energy demands (Scope 2 use)". It covers public supply vs self-supply, groundwater vs surface water where possible, ML-based depiction of uncertainty, NLP over SCOPUS abstracts, and review of "industry reports, policy, media publications, and utility filings". Objective 3 is a USGS Water Mission Area–Water Science Center collaboration with a regional entity. — [Zintellect DOI-USGS-2026-33](https://www.zintellect.com/Opportunity/Details/DOI-USGS-2026-33)
- USGS publishes linked heat-and-water-budget estimates of thermoelectric withdrawal and consumption that are independent of operator reports, and monthly HUC12 ML-based estimates for public supply (2000-2020) and thermoelectric use (2008-2020). The AWUDS archive covers 1985-2015. — [USGS thermoelectric estimates (data.gov)](https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/water-withdrawal-and-consumption-estimates-for-thermoelectric-power-plants-in-the-united-s-09977); [USGS public supply/irrigation/thermoelectric trends](https://www.usgs.gov/data/trends-modelled-public-supply-irrigation-and-thermoelectric-water-use-across-conterminous); [USGS water use 1985-2015](https://www.usgs.gov/data/water-use-data-various-spatial-scales-united-states-1985-2015)

**Other funded projects**
- Minnesota Sea Grant 2026 Fast-Track grant, "Decision-Support Tool for AI Impact on Minnesota's Water": estimates a data center's real-time water use from equipment and operations, predicts heat and sulfate effects on water systems, and builds a dashboard for compliance with Minnesota HF16. — [Minnesota Sea Grant](https://seagrant.umn.edu/research/ai-water-impact)
- NSF 2450515, DESC Type I, "Water- and Carbon-Aware Design of Chiplet-based Systems ... for AI in Datacenters" (Arizona State, PI Arora, 2025-08, $600,000) is a computing-hardware design project, not hydrology. NSF 2324514, "Sustainable Serverless Computing" (Colorado State, Pasricha, 2023), is also computing-side. An NSF Award API keyword search for "data center(s)"/"datacenter"/"AI infrastructure" since 2022 found no hydrology-side data center water-use estimation award (66 awards screened). — [NSF Award API query](https://api.nsf.gov/services/v1/awards.json?keyword=%22data%20center%22); [NSF 2450515](https://www.nsf.gov/awardsearch/showAward?AWD_ID=2450515)
- PNNL report (2026), Catalano, Duan, Quimby, Wang, Chini, Premathilake, McPherson et al., "Synergistic Pathways for Water Security in Emerging Sectors: Data Centers and Modern Nuclear Facilities": covers stand-alone data centers, nuclear-powered data centers and SMRs as water-intensive sectors needing integrated planning. — [PNNL](https://www.pnnl.gov/publications/synergistic-pathways-water-security-emerging-sectors-data-centers-and-modern-nuclear)
- An Argonne study (announced March 2026) on nuclear–data center co-location names water availability as a criterion and plans siting case studies. — [BusinessWire](https://businesswire.com/news/home/20260318862698/en/Are-Nuclear-Power-Plants-the-Key-to-Powering-Our-Digital-World-Argonne-Study-Aims-to-Find-Out)

**Literature**
- Siddik, Shehabi & Marston (2021, Environmental Research Letters), "The environmental footprint of data centers in the United States": the first spatially detailed US data center carbon and water footprint. About 20% of the direct water footprint comes from moderately to highly stressed watersheds. Water scarcity footprint 1.29×10⁹ m³-eq vs operational water footprint 5.13×10⁸ m³. — [LBNL record](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/bibcite/reference/34978); [VT dataset](https://data.lib.vt.edu/articles/dataset/The_environmental_footprint_of_data_centers_in_the_United_States/14504913)
- LBNL 2024 United States Data Center Energy Usage Report (Shehabi et al.): covers electricity and water with scenarios through 2028. Secondary sources cite about 17.4 billion gal (about 66 billion L) of direct use in 2023 and about 800 billion L (211 billion gal) of indirect use. The water numbers were not verified in the PDF in this session. — [LBNL](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/publications/2024-lbnl-data-center-energy-usage-report); [secondary: Insurance Journal 2026-10-13](https://www.insurancejournal.com/news/national/2026/10/13/888682.htm)
- Li, Yang, Islam & Ren, "Making AI Less 'Thirsty'" (arXiv 2304.03271, v1 2023 to v5 2025): location- and time-varying water footprint of AI. GPT-3 training could evaporate 700,000 L. AI could account for 4.2–6.6 billion m³ of withdrawal in 2027. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.03271v4)
- Han, Li, Wierman & Ren, "Small Bottle, Big Pipe" (arXiv 2603.02705, 2026-03): direct withdrawals and their effect on public water systems. Peak-day capacity is the binding constraint. US data centers could need 697–1,451 MGD of new water capacity through 2030 (227–604 MGD with a 10%/yr intensity reduction). Recommends reporting peak water use and "Pipe Neutral" coordinated water-power planning. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02705)
- Akinade, Amanambu, Frame & Ren, "AI Data Centers and the Water Use Feedback Loop" (arXiv 2606.21760, 2026-06): a review introducing a Water Consumption Impact index. Across ten US sites the burden spans 0.2%–134% of host capacity. A secondary summary says it argues that peak cooling demand coincides with low streamflow and cites a "Drought Amplification Factor". — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.21760); [search summary of PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.21760)
- Bolaños-Zuñiga & Lamadrid, "Environmental and Economic Implications of AI Data Centers in the US" (arXiv 2608.09882, 2026-08): finds that impacts depend on marginal generation mixes, water availability and land use, and that regulation does not fully capture location- and time-specific externalities. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.09882)
- An ACS Environmental Science & Technology study (press release October 2026) mapped 9,347 operational data centers worldwide. About 25% are in areas with water stress for at least one month a year, rising to about one-third under roughly 3 °C warming. — [ACS press release](https://www.acs.org/pressroom/presspacs/2026/october/many-data-centers-are-located-where-water-resources-are-limited.html)
- WRI developed a method that identifies cooling type and fuel at thermal power plants from free satellite imagery and then applies water-use factors. Tested on 200 US plants: 90% precision for cooling technology, 89% for fuel, 69% for water demand. — [WRI](https://www.wri.org/research/methodology-estimate-water-demand-thermal-power-plants-data-scarce-regions-using-satellite)
- Disclosure quality: an analysis of public records and sustainability reports from Amazon, Google, Microsoft, Meta, Digital Realty and Equinix found voluntary, inconsistent and non-comparable water reporting that often omits indirect water. — [AIhub / The Conversation, 2025-09](https://aihub.org/2025/09/24/data-centers-consume-massive-amounts-of-water-companies-rarely-tell-the-public-exactly-how-much)
- Searches for satellite (Landsat, ECOSTRESS, MODIS) detection or quantification of data center or industrial cooling-tower water consumption found nothing. ECOSTRESS gives 70 m LST and ET products calibrated for vegetation. Its industrial use case is detecting thermal discharge from once-through power plants (Diablo Canyon). — [ECOSTRESS aquatic applications poster](https://ecostress.jpl.nasa.gov/downloads/other/ECOSTRESS_aquatic_applications_poster.pdf)

### Inferences
- The USGS Scope 1 project is both a threat (duplication) and an opportunity: it is the natural USGS partner for the required letter and for Element 4. Hao Lab should position its proposal as supplementing it: Scope 2 plus Tennessee validation plus extreme-weather forecasting. It should not repeat a national Scope 1 inventory or a literature-NLP review.
- The Ren group dominates national and peak-capacity framing. A Tennessee/TVA-specific, validated, uncertainty-quantified, hydro-climate-coupled approach is the differentiable space.
- Memphis (self-supplied aquifer use, municipal reuse) and Knoxville (moratorium) map directly onto the NOFO's named examples (self-supply, reuse) and onto Shi Jin's moratorium-aware siting paper.

## Idea-by-idea screening (gap, novelty, Hao Lab fit, call fit, risks, verdict)

### Takeaway
Pursue Idea 1, sharpened toward Scope 1+2 estimation with ML and uncertainty and
validation in Tennessee, and Idea 3, refocused on seasonal and extreme-weather
forecasting. Revise Idea 2 into image-based cooling-technology detection and
Idea 5 into a data-provenance and bias audit; both work better as components
than as standalone proposals. Drop Idea 4 as a standalone 104g proposal. The
strongest single submission merges 1 + 3 with 2 and 5 as data layers.

#### Idea 1: Tennessee Valley data center water-use estimation and forecasting linking electricity demand to on-site cooling (Scope 1) and off-site thermoelectric (Scope 2) water, validated against withdrawal records
- Closest prior work: national, spatially resolved Scope 1+2 (Siddik et al. 2021); LBNL 2024 national scenarios; Ren group (time- and location-varying water intensity); USGS internal Scope 1-only project.
- Gap and novelty: no ML-based, uncertainty-quantified, facility- or county-level Scope 1+2 estimate for a single balancing authority (TVA) validated against independent records was found. National studies use national or regional average water intensities (see the search summary on EWIF limits). The Scope 2 gap is explicitly left open by the USGS project. Novelty is real but incremental in method, and strong in geography and validation.
- Why us: a submitted paper on water use tied to data center electricity demand (direct precursor); ISSE footprint grant; 104b TDEC project (stakeholder access to TDEC); Gao and Jin's Tennessee Valley siting models supply the demand side.
- Call fit: strong on "estimating, validating, forecasting" and AI/ML. Weaker if Scope 2 dominates, because the NOFO focuses on public-supply facility use. Lead with Scope 1 from public supply and treat Scope 2 as the "associated" component that supplements USGS.
- Risks: access to ground truth (utility billing data held by MLGW, KUB or other utilities; withdrawal records with unverified availability); scope creep for $120K; overlap with the 104b TDEC project, which the program screens for.
- Verdict: **Pursue (sharpen).** Make it a hybrid physics-ML estimator: IT load → cooling technology → WUE, and grid mix → plant-level water intensity, with probabilistic uncertainty. Validate on 3–5 Tennessee facilities with utility or permit data via letters of support. Clearly separate it from the TDEC decision-support deliverable.

#### Idea 2: Remote sensing of cooling water consumption from Landsat, ECOSTRESS or MODIS thermal and ET signatures
- Closest prior work: none found for data centers. WRI's satellite cooling-type classification for power plants is the nearest analog. ECOSTRESS industrial use is limited to once-through thermal discharge.
- Gap and novelty: a real gap, but the physics is weak. Cooling towers are building-scale. Landsat TIRS resamples to about 100 m (from training knowledge, not verified here) and ECOSTRESS is 70 m. ET models are calibrated for vegetated surfaces. Quantifying evaporative loss from thermal or ET signatures is high-risk for a 3-year, $120K project.
- Why us: the PI's ET, remote sensing and MODIS expertise; Shi Jin's computer vision (YOLO/EfficientNet) and infrared + LiDAR fusion.
- Call fit: matches "observing" and "independent validation" and AI/ML. Feasibility is the concern.
- Risks: signal-to-noise; no ground truth for calibration; MODIS (500 m–1 km) is too coarse.
- Verdict: **Revise.** Change it to image-based detection of facilities and cooling technology (cooling towers vs dry or adiabatic coolers vs chillers) from high-resolution aerial or satellite imagery using computer vision, following the WRI power-plant analog. Use Landsat or ECOSTRESS thermal anomalies only as auxiliary operating-status or seasonality signals. Cooling type is the key variable for predicting WUE. Use it as the "observing" layer feeding Idea 1, not as a standalone proposal.

#### Idea 3: Scenario forecasts of future data center water demand under siting policy, combined with flash drought and heatwave stress on local supply
- Closest prior work: "Small Bottle, Big Pipe" (national peak-day capacity to 2030); "Water Use Feedback Loop" (WCI index across ten sites; peak cooling coincides with low flow); ACS ES&T (global stress under +3 °C); news analyses of drought overlap. None couples siting-policy-aware build-out (tariffs, moratoria, interconnection queues) with process-based flash-drought and heatwave hydrology at local supply scale.
- Gap and novelty: real. It combines (a) policy-aware siting scenarios from the lab's own two in-progress papers, (b) weather-driven seasonal and peak cooling demand, and (c) flash-drought and heatwave stress on source water. It directly answers the NOFO's "forecast future scenarios" and "seasonal variability".
- Why us: the PI's flash drought and heatwave work; Jin's moratorium- and tariff-aware siting model; Gao's interconnection-queue robust siting; ORNL climate-modeling collaborators. This pairing is distinctive to this lab.
- Call fit: strong on forecasting, seasonality and uncertainty, and on EO14239 (local preparedness).
- Risks: may read as too "planning" and not enough "observing/validating". Needs an observed-data validation hook from Idea 1. Forecasts are hard to validate within the award period.
- Verdict: **Pursue**, ideally as Aim 2 of a merged proposal with Idea 1. As a standalone proposal it is weaker on validation.

#### Idea 4: Freshwater withdrawals and thermal discharges for data centers co-located with SMRs, building on the 104b TDEC project
- Closest prior work: PNNL 2026 report (data centers + SMRs + water security); Argonne co-location study; the lab's own 104b TDEC project, which has nearly the same title.
- Gap and novelty: there is some gap, but no operating SMR–data center pairs exist to observe or validate in Tennessee (not verified; no such facility was found in searches). The NOFO focuses on public-supply industrial use, not reactor cooling.
- Why us: the 104b TDEC project is a direct asset, but that is also the problem: the NOFO screens for overlap and duplication with funded projects.
- Call fit: weak to moderate. It is not observation or validation of current public-supply use.
- Risks: duplication with the 104b award; no ground truth; off-topic for the FY2026 priority.
- Verdict: **Drop** as a standalone 104g proposal. Cite the 104b TDEC project as the decision-support pathway (Element 7 transfer to users) and as evidence of prior performance.

#### Idea 5: NLP extraction of water-use disclosures from permits, utility filings and sustainability reports to build a validation dataset
