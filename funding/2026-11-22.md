- Official sources: [program page](https://www.orau.org/partnerships/grant-programs/powe/),
  [instructions for faculty](https://www.orau.org/partnerships/grant-programs/powe/faculty-instructions.html),

  [consortium members](https://www.orau.org/partnerships/consortium-members.html)
  (UT Knoxville is a charter member) and the
  [councilor list](https://www.orau.org/partnerships/grant-programs/powe/councilors/index.html).
  The application period closes 2027-01-06, noon EST, in ORAU PeerNet;
  notifications come in early June; the award year runs July 1 - June 30.
- Award: $5,000 from ORAU plus at least $5,000 from the institution; no
  overhead on either. Funds may pay faculty summer salary, graduate student
  salary, travel and equipment. A one- to two-page final report is due within
  a year.
- Eligibility: full-time assistant professors at ORAU member institutions
  "within two years of their initial tenure track appointment (January 1, 2025
  to January 6, 2027)". The FAQ says non-tenure-track "Research Assistant
  Professor" titles are not eligible and only time at the current institution
  counts; there is no citizenship requirement.
- Limited submission: only two nominations per institution. The ORAU
  Councilor (the list names Jill Passano for the University of Tennessee)
  selects up to two junior faculty, requests the chair or dean letter and
  sends the Councilor Support Form and PeerNet link.
- Package: two-page proposal (goals and background; expected outcomes and
  relevance; design and methodology; competence of the applicant; facilities
  and resources; budget justification), one-page resume, one-page
  publications list, optional one-page references, chair or dean letter,
  Councilor Support Form.
- Review: two independent reviews by discipline experts in PeerNet on
  scientific merit, method, the applicant's previous research performance,
  facilities, the likelihood of leading to fundable research or publishable
  work, and overall quality; the ORAU Policy Committee makes the final choice.
- Areas: Engineering and Applied Science; Life Sciences; Mathematics/Computer
  Sciences; Physical Sciences; Policy, Management, or Education; and two
  special topics, "Critical Mineral Constraints on SMR Deployment Capacity in
  the U.S." and "Responsible AI in STEM Education Policy". The special topics
  appear by title only: no description, sponsor or separate review rule is
  given on any official page.
- Fit: score 4, the cap for an open-topic seed award. The SMR special topic
  sits next to the lab's data center + SMR co-location thread (funded USGS
  104b TDEC grant; both students' siting papers).
- Cautions: (1) **Tenure-track check.** The CV lists "Assistant Professor,

  not say the appointment is tenure-track. The 10/2024-07/2026 "Research
  Assistant Professor" role is ineligible and must not be used as the start of
  the clock. Confirm with the ISE Department Head and the Councilor.
  (2) **Internal nomination first.** UT Knoxville's internal deadline could

##### PW1. How many SMRs, where, and when? A mineral-constrained deployment-capacity model for co-locating small modular reactors with AI data centers — Pursue

**Call.** ORAU Powe FY2027, special topic "Critical Mineral Constraints on SMR
Deployment Capacity in the U.S."

**Team.** Yuefeng Hao (PI, sole applicant). Yifan Gao (robust optimization
under uncertainty; NLP information extraction) is paid from the award. Shi Jin
(from Spring 2027; Tennessee Valley siting model) contributes the site layer.
Letters or data guidance, not co-PIs, could come from the recommended
collaborators below; none has been contacted.

**Research question and approach.** Given midstream limits on nuclear-grade
zirconium, hafnium, nickel-chromium-molybdenum alloys and HALEU, how much SMR
capacity can the U.S. deploy by 2035, which candidate data center co-location
sites should receive constrained module deliveries first, and which mineral's
shadow price binds? Months 1-3: per-module mineral intensities and supply
scenarios from Rhodium/Rovjok, USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2026 and HALEU
projections, with Gao's NLP building a source-linked supply table (PW10).
Months 3-8: a multi-period, scenario-based deployment model with deliveries
capped by mineral supply, reusing Gao's robust-optimization comparison; sites
screened with published OR-SAGE criteria and Shi Jin's Tennessee Valley inputs.
Months 8-12: deployable GW, unmet data center load, mineral shadow prices and
regret across scenarios; one journal paper. Budget: mostly graduate summer
support, plus travel to present (for example INFORMS or ANS).

**Closest existing projects and papers, and the gap.**
[Rhodium/Rovjok 2026](https://rhg.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/Critical-Mineral-Chokepoints-Could-Threaten-the-US-Nuclear-Energy-Renaissance.pdf)
quantifies national mineral needs for a Gen 3+ build-out (at least 18 USGS
critical minerals; about 3,300 t of zirconium for a doubled fleet; zirconium
at 8.1% of ex-China production) and says the outlook is "more nuanced" for
Gen 4 SMRs at scale, but has no time-phased deployment, siting or data center
link. The [Payne Institute](https://payneinstitute.mines.edu/nuclear-and-smr-non-fuel-critical-minerals-supply-chain-an-emerging-fourth-value-chain/)
piece is qualitative and framed for Canada. The ANL/INL/ORNL "Preliminary
Analysis of Nuclear-Powered Data Center Scenarios" sites and sizes
nuclear-powered data centers with OR-SAGE water and exclusion layers but has
no mineral cap on build rates ([OSTI 3001074](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/3001074);
inferred from its contents and keyword hits). The UT CURENT SMR-hyperscale
microgrid project covers electrical operations
([CURENT 2026 report](https://curent.utk.edu/materials/2026-industry-conference/reports/Tolbert_Project_Report_2026_PE.pdf)).
NSI/Bain and OECD NEA build-rate studies are national and component- or
labor-based (search summaries only). An NSF Award API query found no award on
SMR deployment under mineral constraints. **Gap:** in the sources checked, no
work links mineral midstream constraints to time-phased, site-resolved SMR
deployment for data center loads.

**Novelty.** It treats the special topic's "deployment capacity" as an
optimization quantity with mineral shadow prices and robust allocation, not as
an inventory of requirements, and adds data center co-location as the demand
side.

**Why us.** PI of the funded USGS 104b grant "Decision Support for TDEC to
Reduce Freshwater Withdrawals and Thermal Discharges from Data Centers and
Small Modular Reactors in Tennessee" ($84K, from 01/2027) and the UT ISSE
local-footprint grant ($60K, 08/2026); submitted paper "Regional screening of
electricity-related water consumption for projected data-center load growth";
ongoing papers by Gao ("Robust Data Center Siting under Interconnection-Queue
Uncertainty") and Shi Jin ("Siting Under Moving Rules"); Gao's NLP background; the
PI's ISE appointment and DOE/ORNL grant (PI).

**Fundability.** A knowledge gap tied to the special topic's own wording;
standard OR methods the students already use; computing-only facilities; one
paper is plausible in 12 months; seeds NSF CAREER **K1** (a supply-side
constraint on regional siting) and CMMI **C1**. The weak point is the
applicant-record criterion (no minerals or nuclear-materials record): state
that the mineral data are public USGS and Rhodium figures and name the ORNL
and UT nuclear collaborations in the text, which the FAQ encourages.

**Risks.** Two UT slots and an unknown internal deadline; the special topic
may be a single all-or-nothing award; reviewers may see the PI as a newcomer
to nuclear materials; SMR mineral intensities and nuclear-grade midstream data
are thin (use scenario ranges); $10K buys about one student summer; one
sentence must separate PW1 from 104b (104b covers withdrawals and thermal
discharges for TDEC; PW1 has no water modeling); eligibility depends on the
tenure-track status.

**Verdict: Pursue.** Request the UT nomination for PW1 now. Before the
internal deadline, ask the Councilor and the ORAU program office whether
special-topic proposals are reviewed separately and whether extra letters can
be uploaded.

##### PW2. Does co-location survive a flash drought? Time-varying cooling-water feasibility for SMR-powered AI data centers in the Tennessee Valley — Revise

**Call.** ORAU Powe FY2027, Engineering and Applied Science.

**Team.** Yuefeng Hao (PI); Yifan Gao (siting optimization, paid as a
student); Shi Jin's Tennessee Valley siting inputs.

**Research question and approach.** Static siting screens treat cooling water
as an exclusion layer. How much SMR-data center co-location capacity at
candidate Tennessee Valley sites stays feasible during observed flash-drought
and heatwave events, by cooling type? Take candidate sites and cooling types
from published OR-SAGE criteria; detect historical events with the PI's
heatwave and flash-drought methods; compute event-wise feasibility and
capacity at risk; compare static and dynamic screens. PW6 (heat coincidence of
cooling peaks and derates) becomes an aim.

**Closest existing projects and papers, and the gap.** ANL/INL/ORNL 2025
([PDF](https://www.osti.gov/servlets/purl/3001074)) and the
[OR-SAGE methods manuscript](https://nric.inl.gov/content/uploads/34/2024/12/Methods-and-system-for-siting-advanced-nuclear-reactors-and-evaluating-energy-policy-concerns.pdf)
use static water and exclusion layers; the CURENT project covers electrical
operations. Event-resolved hydro-climate feasibility of co-location capacity
was not found in the sources checked.

**Novelty.** Brings flash-drought and heatwave event science into
nuclear-data center siting screens.

**Why us.** Heatwave hydrology across CONUS (*J. Hydrol.* 637, 2024);
flash-drought WUE and recovery (*AFM* 378, 2026); flash-drought and recovery
papers (2022-2023); the funded 104b and ISSE data center grants; the students'
siting papers.

**Fundability.** The strongest applicant-record match of all ideas and a
direct path to CAREER **K1** and CBET EWRE **E1**, but reviewers or the
Councilor may see it as current funding repackaged: 104b covers the same
basin, sector pair and resource, and runs alongside the Powe year.

**Risks.** Overlap with 104b (from 01/2027) and with the dropped tracked idea
U5; the two-nomination limit.

**Verdict: Revise.** Use only if PW1 cannot go forward. Reframe as pure
hydro-climate feasibility science, with no TDEC decision tool and no
thermal-discharge modeling, and state the 104b boundary explicitly.

##### PW3. From permission to practice: evidence-based responsible-AI course policy for engineering management education — Revise (hold)

**Call.** ORAU Powe FY2027, special topic "Responsible AI in STEM Education
Policy" (or Policy, Management, or Education).

**Team.** Yuefeng Hao (PI); Yifan Gao (NLP on policy texts, paid as a
student).

**Research question and approach.** How do AI-use policies in engineering
courses at Tennessee universities differ, and does a structured
responsible-AI policy (disclosure, verification and attribution rules) change
student outcomes in an AI-intensive engineering management course? Collect
public syllabi and institutional guidance and classify them with NLP; run a
policy pilot in IE 591 and IE 465/565 with human-subjects approval; write a
policy brief.

**Closest existing projects and papers, and the gap.** An ASEE 2026 study of
generative-AI policies of 169 engineering faculty at 18 institutions
([ASEE](https://nemo.asee.org/public/conferences/374/papers/52286/download)),
an analysis of 98 computing syllabi from 54 R1 institutions
([arXiv 2410.22281](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.22281)) and a study of
institutional guidance ([arXiv 2402.01659](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.01659))
describe policies (all seen as search summaries). The possible gap, linking
policy design to measured learning in engineering management courses, was not
verified as absent.

**Novelty.** Limited: an outcome test of course policy in one course type.

**Why us.** The PI teaches IE 591 (Fall 2026), IE 465/565 (Spring 2027) and
IE 200; SeER Teacher Award (2025); pending USDA NIFA HEC proposal on AI in
agricultural systems education (PI, $300K); Gao's NLP background. There are no
education-research publications in the talent pool.

**Fundability.** Matches a special topic, but the applicant-record criterion
is weak.

**Risks.** Crowded literature; IRB timing within 12 months; a weak-record
proposal is a poor use of one of two UT slots; overlap must be stated if the
HEC proposal is funded.

**Verdict: Revise (hold).** Do not use the UT nomination on PW3; keep it as
material for the CAREER K1 education plan and the HEC project.

##### PC1. Water for the AI build-out: a national synthesis of data center and power-sector water withdrawal, consumption and thermal discharge under drought and heat — Pursue

**Call.** USGS Powell Center working group, due 2027-01-31 (keywords: water;
energy and minerals; climate and land use change).

**Team.** Required USGS PI: a USGS water-use scientist (best candidate below:
the thermoelectric and industrial water-use modeler at the LMG Water Science
Center in Nashville). Co-PI: Yuefeng Hao. Co-PI: a data center
water-footprint academic (for example the past Powell water-use PI below);
optional fourth PI from a national lab. Participants: Yifan Gao and Shi Jin
(facility layer, NLP and ETL), registry collaborators Jiafu Mao and Mingzhou
Jin, a utility or state water agency participant, and a policy participant.
Fellow: a postdoc at UT through the $100K Fellow route, the only money to UT.
Nobody has been contacted.

**Research question and approach.** Where and when does AI data center growth,
counting direct cooling water plus water at the power plants that supply it,
push withdrawals, consumption and thermal discharges past what drought-year
and heatwave-year streamflow and river temperatures can absorb? How large is
data center water use at HUC12-month scale, how much is indirect, and which
basins move from low to high stress in dry and hot years? Existing data only:
(1) a harmonized national facility layer from the inventories behind Siddik
et al. 2021 and the LBNL reports, plus text-mined public permits and utility
filings; (2) links to the generators that serve each facility and to the USGS
thermoelectric plant-month reanalysis (2008-2020;
[doi:10.5066/P9ZE2FVM](https://doi.org/10.5066/P9ZE2FVM)) and condenser-duty
data ([doi:10.5066/P9XG876W](https://doi.org/10.5066/P9XG876W)), updated with
the automated model code; (3) direct use placed within the USGS public-supply
HUC12 reanalysis; (4) a stress test against USGS streamflow and temperature
records and NWAA water budgets for the driest and hottest years, using the
PI's flash-drought and heatwave event definitions; (5) ML gap-filling with
uncertainty bounds. Products: a USGS data release, a synthesis paper and a
screening framework state agencies could use in permitting.

**Closest existing projects and papers, and the gap.** The 2019 Powell
water-use group fused economic and water-use data by county and watershed
before the AI load surge and with no data center category
([page](https://www.usgs.gov/centers/john-wesley-powell-center-for-analysis-and-synthesis/science/reanalyzing-and-predicting-us)).
The USGS thermoelectric model covers 1,341 plants monthly for 2008-2020, not
the loads that drive them ([Harris et al. 2025](https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/70262012)).
The USGS 2020 industrial water-use release covers manufacturing (NAICS 31-33)
only, per a search summary of its metadata
([data page](https://www.usgs.gov/data/industrial-water-use-2020-county-huc12-month-and-year-conterminous-united-states));
no USGS estimate that breaks out data centers was found. Siddik, Shehabi and
Marston 2021 is an annual, pre-AI-boom footprint without a drought-year test
([LBNL record](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/publications/environmental-footprint-data-centers)).
Han et al. 2026 focus on peak demand on public water systems, without basin
hydrology or thermal discharge ([arXiv 2603.02705](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.02705),
abstract only). National totals conflict over definitions and boundaries
([Pacific Institute](https://pacinst.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/09/Issue-Brief_AI-Data-Centers-2026_Final.pdf);
[ITIF](https://www2.itif.org/2026-data-centers-water.pdf); search summaries).
**Gap:** no national, basin-scale, monthly synthesis joins direct and indirect
data center water with drought and heat supply limits and thermal discharge.

**Novelty.** The first national data center water-use layer consistent with
USGS water-use models, and a drought- and heat-conditioned stress test that
turns a contested annual national number into basin-month risk; the 2012
shale-gas group is the precedent for synthesizing a new energy sector's water
effects nationally.

**Why us.** PI of the funded USGS 104b TDEC grant (withdrawals and thermal
discharges from data centers and SMRs in Tennessee) and the funded UT ISSE
local-footprint grant; submitted paper on electricity-related water
consumption for projected data center load growth; flash-drought and heatwave
papers (*AFM* 378, 2026; *J. Hydrol.* 637, 2024) for the stress definitions;
Gao's NLP extraction from policy documents and Shi Jin's 350K-record ETL and
retrieval platform for the facility layer; the PI's experience as Topic Lead
of the DOE RUBISCO Soil Moisture Working Group mini-workshops (2023-2025).

**Fundability.** Strong on urgency (load growth now; conflicting national
numbers), outcomes (data release plus framework), "why Powell" (data split
across USGS models, LBNL inventories, utility and state filings and academic
footprints, as in the 2019 group) and unique contribution. Expertise is strong
only with the USGS water-use PI and a footprint academic on board. Data
availability is the main risk, because facility water data are partly
proprietary.

**Risks.** No USGS PI yet; it could read as "should be funded by another
entity" (answer: synthesis of existing USGS and LBNL data that needs
convening); overlap with the funded 104b grant and tracked ideas 104g
**U1/U2**, 104(b) **I3**, BER **B1**, EWRE **E1/E2**, CAREER **K1/K2** (keep
Powell national and existing-data-only, with no Tennessee decision tool);
commercially sensitive facility data (aggregate to HUC12 and county); no money
for PI time; an early-career PI (pair with a past Powell PI).

**Verdict: Pursue.** Contact the USGS water-use scientist and the interim
director in early December; ask whether a data center water synthesis fits the
USGS mission and whether co-sponsorship (for example DOE or LBNL) is welcome.

##### PC2. Fast in, slow out? A continental synthesis of flash drought onset speed and ecosystem water-carbon recovery — Pursue (alternate)

**Call.** Same (keywords: ecosystems; water; natural hazards; climate and land
use change).

**Team.** USGS PI options below (Southeast CASC Science Coordinator; South
Atlantic WSC flash-drought PI; EROS SSEBop lead and past Powell PI). Co-PI:
Yuefeng Hao. Third co-PI: a flash-drought or flux community lead. Participants:
registry collaborators Jiafu Mao, Yaoping Wang, Daniel Ricciuto, Lianhong Gu
and Jeffrey Wood; Shi Jin (data pipeline). Fellow: a postdoc at UT. Nobody has
been contacted.

**Research question and approach.** Across North American biomes, do droughts
that develop fast leave longer or shorter water-carbon recovery than slow
droughts of equal depth, and does climate history or ecosystem type explain
the difference? Detect events consistently from soil-moisture and ET
anomalies (SSEBop, MODIS, soil-moisture networks); pair each with
AmeriFlux/FLUXNET GPP, ET and WUE and satellite greenness; estimate recovery
times with the PI's recovery-time and triple-collocation methods; separate
onset speed from severity, timing and biome with hierarchical models and ML.
Products: a public event-recovery database and a manager-oriented "recovery
debt" indicator.

**Closest existing projects and papers, and the gap.** Zhang and Yuan 2020
found 151 flash-drought events at 29 FLUXNET sites with GPP below normal
within 16 days and recovery lagging soil moisture, but made no fast-versus-slow
comparison ([HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/24/5579/2020/)). The
2020 and 2024 Powell groups covered imposed multi-year drought and
diversity-climate-history stability, mostly with biological community data.
NSF CAIG 2616279/2616278 forecasts flash-drought development, not recovery
([NSF 2616279](https://www.nsf.gov/awardsearch/showAward?AWD_ID=2616279)); NSF
2303458 studies land-atmosphere feedbacks
([NSF 2303458](https://www.nsf.gov/awardsearch/showAward?AWD_ID=2303458)). An
AmeriFlux 22-tower drought and heat synthesis was seen only in search results.
**Gap:** no synthesis has tested onset speed against recovery across networks
(moderate confidence; the search was not exhaustive).

**Novelty.** Turns flash drought from a forecasting topic into a
recovery-legacy question, with a reusable event database.

**Why us.** Flash-drought WUE and recovery (*AFM* 378, 2026); drought recovery
of carbon and water fluxes (*J. Hydrol.* 622, 2023); flash-drought ecosystem
effects (2023); drought-index recovery time (*SERRA* 2022); triple
collocation (2022); PT, CR-BMA and partitioning ET methods; FFAR flash-drought
tool (Co-PI); ORNL co-authors; RUBISCO topic lead.

**Fundability.** Strong on data availability and methods; moderate on unique
contribution, since reviewers may see it as adjacent to the 2020 and 2024
groups; urgency from Southeast events and the SE CASC manager need.

**Risks.** USGS PI must agree; overlap with NSF GEO **G1**, 104(b)
**I1/I2**, CLaSH **CL1/CL3** and, in this scan, BIO **BE1**; a second Powell
proposal with the same PI in one year may be unwise.

**Verdict: Pursue as the alternate,** or hold for the FY2029 round if PC1 goes
in. Sharpen the fast-versus-slow framing and name the SE CASC user need.

##### PC3. When does dry become dangerous? Flash drought and heat thresholds for large wildfires in the humid Southeast — Revise

**Call.** Same.

**Team.** A USGS fire-science PI is needed (none identified as available; the
Southeast CASC fire ecologist is listed as a former employee), or the South
Atlantic WSC hydrologist whose project names the 2016 Smokies fires; Yuefeng
Hao (co-PI); Jiafu Mao (registry; Southeast fire review co-author).

**Research question and approach.** Combine fire perimeters and records with
ET, soil-moisture and streamflow anomalies and heatwave metrics for past
Southeastern fire seasons (2007, 2011, 2016-type events) to find compound
thresholds and lead times.

**Closest existing projects and papers, and the gap.** The 2016 fall drought
was the second most severe since at least 1895 and the Gatlinburg fires burned
more than 11,000 ha ([Williams et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5956230));
a NIDIS-funded [2016 drought and wildfire assessment](https://drought.gov/drought-research/2016-southeast-drought-and-wildfire-assessment)
and the CASC "[Future of Fire](https://www.usgs.gov/programs/climate-adaptation-science-centers/science/future-fire-towards-a-national-synthesis)"
national synthesis exist; the Powell fire-scar network is continental. A
multi-season compound threshold synthesis for the humid Southeast was not
found, but the search was narrow.

**Novelty.** Moderate: compound flash-drought-heat thresholds for humid-region
fire.

**Why us.** Flash-drought and heatwave hydrology papers; the Southeast fire
review (*Earth-Science Reviews*, under review); the wildfire-power grid review
(*RSER* 232, 2026).

**Fundability.** Regional scope and no fire-data owner weaken expertise and
unique contribution.

**Risks.** No USGS fire PI; small scope next to continental precedents.

**Verdict: Revise.** Recruit a USGS fire PI and widen to humid-region fire
(Southeast plus Northeast and Upper Midwest), or fold it into PC2 as a hazard
aim. Do not submit this round without a USGS fire scientist.

(stability boundaries, SCUC with flexibility, colocation energy management)

inferred from abstracts.

##### P1. Hosting AI loads in a river-cooled grid: hydro-climate-conditioned hosting capacity and flexibility envelopes for large loads under compound heat-drought stress — Pursue (lead)

**Call.** NSF ECCS EPCL (NSF 26-514).

**Team.** Yuefeng Hao (PI or co-lead: compound extremes, ET and drought
science, data center water); Yifan Gao (robust and scenario optimization,
queue uncertainty); Shi Jin (from Spring 2027: optimization; computer vision
for the P3 thrust). A UT power-systems co-PI is required (candidates below;
none contacted). Mingzhou Jin (registry) as a possible optimization
collaborator.

**Research question and approach.** When a flash drought-heatwave hits a grid
whose thermoelectric and hydro fleet is cooled by one river system, supply
derates and demand rises together. How much firm and flexible data center load
can each bus safely host, and what flexibility envelope should a planner
require, conditional on the hydro-climate regime? Thrust 1: regime-conditioned,
spatially correlated scenarios of load, river temperature and flow, and plant
derates from observations and reanalysis, using the PI's flash-drought and
heatwave methods. Thrust 2: distributionally robust or chance-constrained
hosting capacity with ambiguity sets indexed by regime and flexibility
envelopes (firm, pause, shift) as decision variables; tractable
reformulations, a decomposition, and a bound on the value of regime-awareness
over weather-blind screening. Thrust 3: validation on synthetic TVA-like test
systems (CEII limits), against a weather-blind planner-initiated screen.
Optional thrusts: P3 and P4.

**Closest existing projects and papers, and the gap.** Kim, Dong and Xie site
data centers planner-first with "reliability-gated screening" and
"standardized flexibility envelopes (firm, pause, and shift)" on a synthetic
2,000-bus Texas system, without hydro-climate stress or cooling-water limits
([arXiv 2605.14714](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.14714)). Xie et al. frame large
AI data centers by load-realization uncertainty, without weather- or
water-driven supply risk ([arXiv 2610.14264](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14264)).
EPCL's data center awards cover stability, SCUC flexibility and colocation, with
no hydro-climate or cooling-water constraints (Past winners). Water-constrained
capacity expansion exists without data centers
([PNNL summary](https://www.pnnl.gov/publications/implications-water-constraints-electricity-capacity-expansion-united-states);
[Sandia ERCOT](https://www.sandia.gov/research/publications/details/water-and-climate-impacts-on-ercot-long-term-systems-assessment-2021-12-31/);
search summaries). NERC's 2026 summer assessment lists extreme heat and
drought-driven hydro losses as threats
([summary](https://energycentral.com/energy-management/post/executive-summary----nerc-2026-summer-reliability-assessment-cl8wkhRf4ANF9ZO)),
and TVA expects 9-33 GW of load by 2040
([WUNC](https://www.wunc.org/2026-08-25/tva-welcomes-data-centers-but-at-a-price)).
**Gap:** no study found quantifies hosting capacity for data center load under
coinciding heat-drought derates (web searches; Scopus not queried).

**Novelty.** Hosting capacity made conditional on correlated hydro-climate
regimes, with the flexibility envelope chosen by the optimization rather than
fixed: a new decision-under-uncertainty problem that answers EPCL's "risk
management in the presence of uncertainty" and "growth in data centers".

**Why us.** Heatwave hydrology across CONUS (*J. Hydrol.* 637, 2024);
flash-drought WUE and recovery (*AFM* 378, 2026); drought indices and recovery
time (*SERRA* 36, 2022); PI of the USGS 104b TDEC grant on data center and SMR
withdrawals and thermal discharges and the UT ISSE local-footprint grant;
submitted paper on electricity-related water use for data center load growth;
co-author of "Wildfire-Power Grid Interactions" (*RSER* 232, 2026) with
Fangxing Fran Li, Grant Buster and Mingzhou Jin; the students' queue-uncertainty
and tariff- and moratorium-aware siting models; ORNL E3SM collaborators as a
source of future-climate regimes.

**Fundability.** Intellectual merit: a new problem class with theory
deliverables. Broader impacts: an open hosting-capacity tool for utilities and
regulators, IE 465/565 and IE 591 modules, Tennessee Valley relevance.
Budget about $450K-$600K over 3 years, matching observed EPCL awards.

**Risks.** Panels are power and control theorists and the lab has no record
there (the power co-PI must co-lead and theory must lead the narrative);
overlap with CAREER **K1**, CMMI **C2**, DOE **B1** and NOAA **N1** (sites
only as candidate buses; Earth-system modeling only as an input); plant
cooling data and CEII limits (synthetic grids); fast-moving competitors.

**Verdict: Pursue.** First secure a UT power-systems co-PI (Hector Pulgar or
Fangxing Fran Li; neither contacted), send the one-page summary stating the
planner-side framing, and decide P1 versus K1 timing (K1's CAREER deadline is
2027-07-28; if P1 is pending then, K1 must exclude hosting capacity and
flexibility envelopes).

##### P2. Pricing the rush: Stackelberg design of large-load tariffs and interconnection rules with siting-responsive data centers — Pursue (alternative)

**Call.** NSF ECCS EPCL (NSF 26-514).

**Team.** Shi Jin (tariff and moratorium model, lower level); Yifan Gao (queue
uncertainty; NLP extraction of tariff terms); Yuefeng Hao (PI or co-lead;
water and heat stress as cost drivers). A power-systems co-PI and an
energy-economics co-PI are needed (candidates below; none contacted).

**Research question and approach.** A utility or regulator (leader) chooses
upfront charges, minimum demand and contract length, collateral and exit fees,
mandatory flexibility or bring-your-own-generation, and queue-entry readiness
tests; developers (followers) choose whether, where and how big to build and
how flexible to be. How should the leader design these terms to minimize
stranded-asset risk and cost shift to other customers subject to reliability,
when followers re-site across utilities? A bilevel or MPEC model with a
stochastic follower population; theory on existence and computation of
equilibria and on robustness to misspecified follower models; public tariffs
and TVA's 2026 rate as cases, with P8's extraction pipeline as the data
source.

**Closest existing projects and papers, and the gap.** NSF 2603293 studies
colocation and incentive mechanisms, not location-responsive tariff and
queue-rule design ([NSF 2603293](https://www.nsf.gov/awardsearch/showAward?AWD_ID=2603293)).
Bilevel data center papers model operation, not rule design
([arXiv 2604.10998](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.10998);
[arXiv 2606.26328](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.26328)). LBNL/Brattle analyzed 55
large-load tariffs descriptively
([LBNL brief](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/sites/default/files/2026-08/rate_designs_large_loads_2026.08.10.pdf),
search summary); 23 states had an approved large-load tariff as of May 2026
([Sabin Center](https://blogs.law.columbia.edu/climatechange/2026/06/02/data-center-regulation-what-local-governments-should-know-about-large-load-tariffs-and-clean-transition-tariffs/)).
TVA adopted a data center rate effective 2026-10-01 that "includes a new
upfront cost for large customers"
([WUNC](https://www.wunc.org/2026-08-25/tva-welcomes-data-centers-but-at-a-price)).
EPCN/EPCL market-design CAREERs do not address large-load tariffs. **Gap:**
tariff and queue rules designed as control inputs against spatially mobile,
partly speculative large loads.

**Novelty.** The mobility of followers across utilities, which classic retail
tariff design ignores, inside EPCL's "game theory" and "regulatory and
economic structures" language.

**Why us.** Shi Jin's tariff- and moratorium-aware Tennessee Valley siting
model is the follower model, already in progress; Gao's queue-uncertainty
siting paper and NLP background (a planned source-linked dataset of data
center development requirements) supply the data and uncertainty model; the
ISSE grant (noise, water and land-market tradeoffs) and 104b TDEC grant give
local-cost and state-agency context.

**Fundability.** EPCL language fits almost verbatim; ratepayer protection and
reliability as broader impacts; the Tong-Mount award shows EPCL funds
engineer-economist teams.

**Risks.** Mirror image of CMMI **C1** built on the same student model (submit
only one of P2 and C1 per cycle); close to Sloan **SL1** in this scan (see
"Which call should host which idea"); the Cornell group is a direct
competitor; bilevel theory is demanding for a new team; TVA-specific cost data
may not be public.

**Verdict: Pursue as the alternative to P1.** Choose P2 only if a UT energy
economist joins and the CMMI AMDO program director declines C1.

#### Past winners: NSF BIO Ecosystem Science (DEB, now BIO Core / OIE)

ecosystems respond as they do, usually at $0.4-1.3M over 3-5 years, often as
multi-institution collaboratives that pair a field or long-term site with
network synthesis. Recent examples: SCALE-UP on vapor-pressure-deficit effects

([NSF 2529003](https://www.nsf.gov/awardsearch/showAward?AWD_ID=2529003)); an

##### BE1. Does biodiversity buffer ecosystems against rapid-onset extremes? Diversity, canopy structure and the recovery of carbon and water fluxes after flash droughts and heatwaves across NEON — Pursue

**Call.** NSF BIO Core (NSF 26-517), Ecosystem Science.

**Team.** Yuefeng Hao (PI). External co-PIs are candidates only (priority: a
UT Knoxville ecologist and a NEON structural-diversity ecologist; see below).
Shi Jin could contribute a justified computer-vision sub-aim (BE11). Yifan Gao
works on data center siting and is not counted.

**Research question and approach.** H1 (insurance hypothesis applied to
recovery): ecosystems with higher hydraulic-trait and structural diversity
recover GPP and ET faster after flash droughts and heatwaves, not only resist
better. H2: under rapid-onset events diversity controls recovery more than
resistance, opposite to the grassland pattern in which diversity aids
resistance and dominant species shape recovery. H3: canopy structural
diversity from NEON AOP lidar predicts recovery better than species richness.
Methods: event detection at NEON flux sites and collocated AmeriFlux sites;
resistance, recovery-time and WUE metrics from the PI's recovery papers; NEON
plant diversity, woody-vegetation data, AOP lidar structure and
community-weighted hydraulic traits; hierarchical Bayesian models with site
random effects; a small ELM test with ORNL (BE7) and a canopy-stress layer
(BE11).

**Closest existing projects and papers, and the gap.** Anderegg et al. 2018
showed that hydraulic diversity "buffers variation in ecosystem flux during
dry periods" at 40 FLUXNET forest sites (resistance during drought, not
recovery after rapid-onset events; not NEON)
([Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-018-0539-7)). Bazzichetto et
al. 2024 found biodiversity promotes resistance but dominant species shape
recovery in 150 grassland plots (biomass, not fluxes)
([J. Ecol.](https://boris.unibe.ch/198498/1/2024_JEcol_112_1087.pdf)). Liu et
al. 2024 hypothesized that trait diversity reduces GPP sensitivity after
severe (not flash) droughts ([postprint](https://ddd.uab.cat/pub/artpub/2025/318355/Liu_et_al_Nat_CLimate_Change_2024b_postprint.pdf)).
Lu et al. 2025 attribute GPP recovery time after flash droughts in China to
radiation, aridity and temperature, without diversity
([HESS](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/29/613/2025/)). The NEON
structural-diversity award does not address drought recovery
([NSF 2212859](https://www.nsf.gov/awardsearch/showAward?AWD_ID=2212859)), and
a preliminary EGU26 analysis of 71 forest flux sites found structural metrics
did not predict most flux variability, without isolating extremes
([EGU26-12135](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-12135.html)).
**Gap:** no award or paper found tests diversity or structure as a control of
flux recovery after rapid-onset extremes using NEON's collocated flux,
diversity and AOP data (NSF Award API and journal web searches; Google Scholar
not queried).

**Novelty.** Moves the diversity-stability debate from biomass and annual
productivity to sub-seasonal flux recovery after rapid-onset events, and from
species richness to hydraulic and structural diversity measured consistently
by NEON; the resistance-versus-recovery contrast (H2) is a paradigm test.

**Why us.** Flash-drought WUE and recovery from observations and model runs
(*AFM* 378, 2026, with ORNL co-authors); recovery of carbon and water fluxes
after drought (*J. Hydrol.* 622, 2023); flash-drought effects on Korean
ecosystems (*J. Hydrol.* 624, 2023); drought recovery-time index comparison
(*SERRA* 2022); heatwave hydrology across CONUS (*J. Hydrol.* 637, 2024); AGU
2024 talk on flash-drought recovery at the Missouri Ozark AmeriFlux site; ET
partitioning by ecosystem conductance (*J. Hydrol.* 603, 2021); CIVE v1.0
(under second revision); ORNL Earth system modeling collaborators; ESA Asian
Ecology Section Early Career Award (2026) and ESA section Council
Representative. Missing: trait and NEON plant-diversity expertise, which the
co-PI must bring.

**Fundability.** Matches Ecosystem Science themes 2, 3 and 4 and the explicit
encouragement of NEON data; synthesis is allowed; early-career emphasis
applies; a public event-recovery and diversity dataset is a clear product.
Suggested size about $0.9-1.2M over 4-5 years as a collaborative (within the
2026 award range; not stated in the solicitation).

**Risks.** Statistical power (short NEON records; few events per site; pair
with longer AmeriFlux records; count events before drafting); NEON-AmeriFlux
continuity issues; an engineering PI in BIO; overlap with **G1** (keep G1 on
hydrologic thresholds and T/ET in the humid Southeast, BE1 on diversity at
continental scale, and list both in Current and Pending Support); overlap with
the active DOE/ORNL vulnerability grant (observational diversity mechanism
versus model uncertainty); data reuse only at NEON plots ("no non-NEON
research is allowed within NEON-established plots",
[NEON ORNL site](https://www.neonscience.org/field-sites/ornl)).

**Verdict: Pursue.** Send a one-page summary to Living Systems or an
Ecosystem Science program officer that states the G1 partition; secure a UT
Knoxville ecologist co-PI and a NEON structural-diversity co-PI first.

data centers locate? Aim 1: a transparent cost-allocation model of TVA's
wholesale rate and LPC retail rates from public TVA, EIA and LPC data under
IRP scenarios. Aim 2: TVA's rate versus counterfactuals (take-or-pay, exit fees
and collateral, cost-based demand charges, flexibility credits tied to verified
workload shifting), with incidence by customer class and LPC. Aim 3: each
tariff fed into Shi Jin's siting model to show siting response and its feedback
on cost sharing, plus interviews with LPC and state stakeholders on
administrability. Distributional layer from SL6.

**Closest existing projects and papers, and the gap.** The Emory/UTK grant
studies ratepayer costs with interviews, surveys, documents, cost analyses and
ethnography, and compares state regulatory landscapes
([Emory News](https://news.emory.edu/stories/2026/01/esc_sloan_grant_funds_15-01-2026/story.html));
public descriptions do not mention tariff counterfactuals or siting response
(inference). Harvard ELI reviewed nearly 50 utility commission proceedings
([ELI](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/extracting-profits-from-the-public-how-utility-ratepayers-are-paying-for-big-tech/),
search summary); LBNL surveys large-load tariffs descriptively
([LBNL](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/publications/electricity-rate-designs-large-0)).
Zhou et al. model 371 announced Texas projects in ERCOT, not a public-power
cost-of-service system ([arXiv 2611.16095](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16095));
Scalera et al. report residential effects "close to zero in territories served
by nonprofit utilities" ([EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cpr:ceprdp:21854),
search summary), and other analyses find weak or no cost shift, so the
evidence is contested. In Tennessee, areas hosting data centers saw bills rise
1.6% versus 1.3% statewide in 2023-24
([ThinkTennessee Part I](https://www.thinktennessee.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/poweringtheboom-part1_report_thinktn_july2026.pdf)),
and TVA's board created a new data center rate to "protect residential and
manufacturing customers from subsidizing" data center growth
([TVA Board release](https://s205.q4cdn.com/318912997/files/doc_news/2026/Aug/20/20260820-TVA-Board-Press-Release_FINAL.pdf)).
**Gap:** no counterfactual analysis of large-load tariff designs in TVA's
federal wholesale and local-retail structure was found.

**Novelty.** The first counterfactual analysis, as far as these searches show,
of large-load tariffs in TVA's two-tier public-power system, linking cost
incidence by LPC and customer class with endogenous siting response.

**Why us.** Shi Jin's ongoing paper "Siting Under Moving Rules: A Tariff- and
Moratorium-Aware Optimization Model for AI Data Center Location in the
Tennessee Valley"; Gao's queue-uncertainty siting paper and interest in
geo-distributed workload scheduling; the funded ISSE local-footprint and USGS
104b TDEC grants; the submitted electricity-water paper; *RSER* 2026 with
Fangxing Fran Li and Mingzhou Jin; the PI's teaching of IE 591 and IE 200.

**Fundability.** Directly addresses "electricity rate design", "commercial and
industrial use" and "energy affordability", plus governance through TVA and
LPC decisions; U.S.-focused, non-advocacy (evaluates designs, takes no
position), timely and gap-filling; trains two PhD students and links research
with practice through an LPC advisory board; early-career co-lead. Suggested
size: about $250K over 2 years, or $400-500K over 3 years with the
economist's group and a survey (quarterly approval).

**Risks.** Without a real economist co-lead it reads as engineering; overlap
with the Emory/UTK grant at the same university and state (talk to that team
first and propose a quantitative complement); LPC-level rate and load data are
only partly public; no economics publications in the lab; TVA politics (stay
non-advocacy); contested spillover evidence (frame as design analysis); TVA's
rate terms have been seen only in the board release and news, not the tariff
document.

**Verdict: Pursue** as the single Sloan LOI, on three conditions: an
economist co-lead, a prior conversation with the Emory/UTK team, and SL2 and
SL6 folded in as aims.

##### SL3. Moving rules: local data center governance across the TVA region and how siting responds — Pursue (alternative)

**Call.** Sloan E&E, unsolicited LOI.

**Team.** Yuefeng Hao (co-lead); Yifan Gao (NLP and information extraction;
source-linked requirements dataset); Shi Jin (data pipelines; siting model).
Required co-lead: a political scientist or public administration scholar on
local governance (candidates below).

**Research question and approach.** Why do counties and cities in the seven
TVA states adopt moratoria, zoning restrictions, overlay districts or benefit
agreements for data centers (water, noise, rates, land, partisanship or state
preemption), and how does the patchwork redirect development and its costs?
Build a source-linked panel of local actions from minutes, ordinances and
news with human validation and an error audit; estimate event-history and
diffusion models; interview officials in matched cases; feed rule scenarios
into the siting model.

**Closest existing projects and papers, and the gap.** The Stanford Sloan
grant compares states, not localities; trackers count actions but disagree
(from 54 local moratoria to 632 jurisdictions with formal action, depending
on definitions) and do not explain them ([Route Fifty](https://www.route-fifty.com/artificial-intelligence/2026/08/many-localities-have-tried-data-center-moratoriums-tracker-finds/415696/),
search summary); Oregon State studies battery-storage siting outcomes. Nashville
set the state's first local data center zoning restrictions and other
Tennessee officials are adopting temporary moratoria
([ThinkTennessee Part II](https://www.thinktennessee.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/poweringtheboom-part2-policybrief_august2026.pdf)).
**Gap:** no validated local-rule panel that explains adoption and links it to
siting outcomes.

**Novelty.** A local-rule panel inside one wholesale power system, so local
and state rules vary while wholesale supply is shared, linked to a siting
response model.

**Why us.** Gao's NLP background and planned source-linked dataset of data
center development requirements; Shi Jin's moratorium-aware siting paper and
350K-record ETL and retrieval pipeline.

**Fundability.** Addresses "energy system resilience and governance" and
place-based transitions; only partly answers rates and affordability.

**Risks.** Overlap with CMMI **C1**, which plans a hazard model fitted to
moratorium data (Sloan owns the governance dataset and its explanation; C1
uses it only as an input and lists Sloan as pending); data quality of local
records.

**Verdict: Pursue as the alternative** if SL1 cannot secure an economist
co-lead, or as a second LOI only after SL1 is answered.

##### SL9. Who is curtailed first? Emergency curtailment governance as data center load grows — Revise

**Call.** Sloan E&E, unsolicited LOI.

**Team.** Yuefeng Hao (hydro-climate stress); Yifan Gao (workload-shifting
model); a public policy scholar on reliability and VOLL equity (candidate
below); Fangxing Fran Li (registry) as power-systems advisor.

**Research question and approach.** When winter or heat extremes cut thermal
output (TVA "lost more than 6,000 MW of generation" in Winter Storm Elliott,
[ThinkTennessee Part II](https://www.thinktennessee.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/poweringtheboom-part2-policybrief_august2026.pdf)),
who bears curtailment under current TVA and LPC rules and data center
flexibility deals, and which priority rules would be fair and efficient?
Document analysis of curtailment rules, interviews and a stress-scenario model
using the PI's heatwave and flash-drought expertise.

**Closest existing projects and papers, and the gap.** The North Carolina A&T
Sloan grant on VOLL equity (2023, $50K) and the Stanford household-resilience
grant (Sloan database); NOAA **N1** in the lab's tracker. A study of
curtailment priority with growing large loads was not found, but the search
was narrow.

**Novelty.** Moderate: curtailment governance with large flexible loads.

**Why us.** Heatwave hydrology (*J. Hydrol.* 2024); flash-drought recovery
(*AFM* 2026); the *RSER* 2026 wildfire-power grid review; Gao's scheduling
interest.

**Fundability.** Hits "energy system resilience and governance".

**Risks.** May read as "solely related to climate"; overlaps NOAA N1; no
reliability-economics record.

**Verdict: Revise.** Use as SL1's resilience aim (flexibility credits valued
in emergencies) or a later LOI with a social-science lead.

##### SL4. Footprints and consent: a choice experiment tied to measured local footprints — Revise

**Call.** Sloan E&E, unsolicited LOI.

**Team.** Yuefeng Hao, Shi Jin, plus a survey methodologist or environmental
social scientist (candidate below).

**Research question and approach.** How much do water source, cooling type,
noise, land conversion, tax revenue, rate protection and power source (gas,
grid, SMR) shift acceptance among Tennessee Valley residents and officials, and
do measured footprints change stated preferences? A discrete-choice experiment
with attribute levels calibrated to ISSE-grant measurements.

**Closest existing projects and papers, and the gap.** The Utah State Sloan
survey (water, energy use, location and participation as key considerations;
[USU Today](https://www.usu.edu/today/story/utah-survey-uncovers-why-residents-oppose-new-data-centers),
search summary), the UVA Data Center Opinion Project
([UVA](https://nationalsecurity.virginia.edu/research/data-center-opinion-project))
and a Korean willingness-to-accept study. No U.S. choice experiment tied to
measured footprints was found (search summaries).

**Novelty.** Ties stated acceptance to measured local footprints.

**Why us.** The funded ISSE grant on noise, water and land-market tradeoffs
and the funded 104b TDEC grant.

**Fundability.** Sloan's priorities favor rates and governance over
perceptions.

**Risks.** Close to the Utah State topic; the ISSE grant may already include
perception work (scope not detailed in the talent pool), so Sloan must fund
only the survey.

**Verdict: Revise.** Keep as an optional survey module of SL1 (asking about
rate protection and flexibility terms) or move it to another funder.
