  faculty, postdocs and students, travel, equipment, analysis. Not allowed:
  subawards, participant support, computers.
- Eligibility: PIs at U.S. institutions eligible for NSF funding who can serve
  as an NSF PI; "early-career scientists and investigators are strongly
  encouraged to apply."
- Format: cover page, 250-word abstract, 5-page project description (2
  figures), biosketch, current and pending support, optional letters of
  collaboration, budget in the CLaSH Excel template.
- Priority areas: (1) cross-observatory hypothesis testing at the CLaSH
  Hazard Observatories (Alaska, Appalachia, Puerto Rico, Southern California;
  this year cross-observatory work or Puerto Rico/Alaska is favored);
  (2) modeling the hazard cascade (triggers, hillslope-channel coupling,
  runout, flooding; CSDMS/Landlab/BMI; "ML/AI applications ... are especially
  welcome"); (3) remote sensing and AI at scale (InSAR, FAIR AI-ready
  datasets and benchmarks); (4) event-driven investigations and new hazard
  types.
- Review: intellectual merit, broader impacts, relevance (cascade gaps;
  "link two or more processes or observatories"), innovation and
  collaboration (new partnerships), feasibility.
- Fit: the CLaSH strategic plan names soil moisture as a trigger condition and
  "wet–dry cycles" as preconditioning, and says it wants "climatologists and
  civil engineers"; no observatory lists a drought or ET lead. That is Hao
  Lab's strongest area. The Appalachia observatory (Kentucky) is the nearest
  to Tennessee. Hao Lab has no landslide or geomorphology record, so partners
  are essential.

##### CL1. Does flash drought set up or suppress the landslide cascade? Wet-dry preconditioning of rainfall-triggered landslides in Appalachia and Puerto Rico — Pursue (lead)

**Team.** Yuefeng Hao (PI; ET, soil moisture, flash drought); Yifan Gao (data pipeline, ML screening of predictors); Shi Jin (from Spring 2027; image and time-series ML). ORNL collaborators as possible unfunded advisors (unconfirmed). Partners: see "Recommended collaborators" (all unconfirmed; no subawards allowed, so they join by letter or as a same-institution co-PI).

**Research question and approach.** Does the hydro-climate state 1-90 days before an extreme storm (flash-drought onset, ET deficit, root-zone soil-moisture anomaly) shift the rainfall needed to trigger landslides, and does the sign differ between temperate Appalachian and tropical Puerto Rican soils? Hypotheses: drought-wetting cycles lower thresholds in clay-rich regolith through cracks and preferential flow (H1); deep root-zone drying adds storage and raises thresholds (H2); the net effect depends on soil and critical-zone class (H3). Steps: (1) compile existing landslide inventories (Helene, eastern Kentucky 2022, Maria); (2) build daily ET and root-zone soil-moisture histories with the PI's Priestley-Taylor soil-water-index and BMA ET methods plus SMAP and reanalysis; (3) fit antecedent-conditioned rainfall-threshold models per observatory and compare across sites; (4) release the antecedent-wetness cube and code as a FAIR dataset, with a BMI-wrappable threshold function for the CLaSH Modeling Collaboratory.

**Closest existing work and the gap.** Maria landslides were linked to soil moisture 11-13% above average ([GSA Today](https://www.geosociety.org/gsatoday/science/G383A/article.htm), search summary) and Helene landsliding to rain in the days before the storm ([GSA Today 2026](https://www.geosociety.org/gsa-today/january-2026/insights-into-widespread-landsliding-in-southern-appalachia-from-hurricane), search summary): single events, wet anomalies, days not months. A 2026 paper adds SMAP soil moisture to highway landslide thresholds but does not test drought preconditioning or compare sites ([NHESS 26, 3163](https://nhess.copernicus.org/articles/26/3163/2026/nhess-26-3163-2026.pdf), search summary). Puerto Rican hillslopes "aren't always wet" ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/hillslopes-humid-tropical-climates-arent-always-wet-implications-hydrologic-response), search summary). NSF RAPID 2629285 collects Helene debris-flow field data, not antecedent hydro-climate ([NSF](https://www.nsf.gov/awardsearch/showAward?AWD_ID=2629285)); NSF keyword searches for drought or evapotranspiration plus landslide found no award on drought preconditioning. Inside CLaSH, soil-moisture threshold work focuses first on Puerto Rico and Southern California. **Gap:** a drought and ET history (weeks to months) tested as a preconditioning variable across temperate and tropical observatories, including Appalachia.

**Why us.** Soil-water-index Priestley-Taylor ET and complementary-relationship BMA ET (*AFM* 279, 2019); triple-collocation drought-index integration and drought recovery time (*SERRA* 36, 2022); flash-drought WUE and recovery (*AFM* 378, 2026); heatwave hydrology (*J. Hydrol.* 637, 2024); lead author of the soil-moisture review (*npj CAS* 8, 2025); Co-PI on the FFAR multi-source flash drought and flood framework; Topic Lead of the RUBISCO Soil Moisture Working Group mini-workshops. This is the hydro-climate expertise CLaSH says it wants and does not yet have at the observatories.

**Fit to review criteria and past-winner patterns.** Links two processes (drought/ET → rainfall triggering) and two observatories; new discipline and new partnerships; existing data only, so feasible in 12 months and $40K; FAIR dataset, graduate training, and a course module as broader impacts; remote sensing + modeling + AI tied to an observatory, like both known winners.

**Risks.** Many landslides lack exact failure dates (aggregate by storm); satellite soil moisture is coarse and shallow (use ET-based root-zone proxies and show uncertainty); few events per site (add a regional Kentucky storm catalog); reviewers may see overlap with CLaSH's own soil-moisture task (talk to the observatory leads first); the PI has no landslide record (partner letters are essential).

**Verdict: Pursue.** Before writing: ask the CLaSH program contact for the funded-project list; contact the Appalachia observatory partner and a Puerto Rico observatory researcher for inventory access and letters; confirm UT's indirect rate.

##### CL3. How long does a landslide cascade last? Satellite ecohydrologic recovery of landslide and debris-flow scars across three event ages — Pursue (alternative lead)

**Team.** Yuefeng Hao (PI; recovery-time methods); Shi Jin (scar segmentation and change detection, multi-sensor registration); Yifan Gao (pipeline, statistics).

**Research question and approach.** After landslides strip vegetation, how long do greenness, ET, and water-use efficiency take to recover, what controls it (climate, soil, scar size, later drought), and does slow recovery predict reactivation (some Maria slopes reportedly failed again in Fiona)? Build Landsat/Sentinel-2/MODIS vegetation and ET time series on scars and paired controls from existing inventories; estimate recovery time with the PI's drought-recovery framework; release a FAIR scar-recovery dataset across observatories.

**Gap.** The strategic plan asks "How long do cascading hazards last?" and names vegetation loss and regrowth, but lists no recovery-tracking task. Inventories map where landslides happened, not how they recover. A literature search on satellite recovery of landslide scars has **not** been done (such studies exist for earthquake landslides), so novelty is unproven.

**Why us.** Recovery of carbon and water fluxes after drought (*J. Hydrol.* 622, 2023); drought recovery time (*SERRA* 36, 2022); flash-drought WUE and recovery (*AFM* 378, 2026); Shi Jin's YOLO/EfficientNet detection and infrared + LiDAR registration experience.

**Risks.** Novelty unproven; small scars are below Landsat/MODIS resolution; Helene scars are young.

**Verdict: Pursue as the alternative.** Do not submit both. Choose CL3 only if the literature check confirms the gap and a Puerto Rico ecology partner joins.
