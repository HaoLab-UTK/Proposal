## 1. Past winners: what Sloan E&E has funded on data centers, large loads, rates, local governance, affordability and the Southeast

### Takeaway
Sloan E&E has already funded at least six data-center-related grants since 2025, and the largest one, Emory with UT Knoxville ($1M), covers Tennessee, ratepayer electricity costs, site mapping and regulatory comparison. A Hao Lab LOI therefore cannot be "data centers in Tennessee and who pays" in general. It must bring a quantitative, rate-design or governance question the funded teams do not answer, co-led with a social scientist. The open gaps are counterfactual tariff design and cost incidence inside TVA's public-power system, flexibility terms for large loads, local rule dynamics across the seven-state TVA region, and who is curtailed in grid emergencies. Sloan wants an interdisciplinary social-science core, early-career leads, graduate students, a practitioner advisory board and broad dissemination.

**Detail on the Emory / UT Knoxville grant (the closest funded project)**
- Three-year, $1M project. PI Kristin Phillips (Emory, associate professor of anthropology), co-PIs Jola Ajibade (Emory, environmental sciences) and Gabe Schwartzman (UT Knoxville, assistant professor of geography and sustainability). Consultants: Cathy Kunkel (Institute for Energy Economics and Financial Analysis) and Nikki Luke (UT Knoxville). It studies "effects of data centers on electricity costs for individual ratepayers, structural changes to how energy is supplied and consumed, and issues of environmental and social justice," with case studies of Georgia, Virginia and Tennessee. Methods: "interviews, surveys, official documents, cost analyses, focus groups and ethnography." Existing and planned sites "will be mapped and made available online" and overlaid with demographic data. A "comparative analysis of the regulatory and policy landscapes across the three states" will identify best practices — [Emory News](https://news.emory.edu/stories/2026/01/esc_sloan_grant_funds_15-01-2026/story.html)
- The UT side "will study how utilities such as the Tennessee Valley Authority respond to increased energy demand and the social implications of new energy infrastructure." It will report "on the energy demands of data centers for specific communities so residents can understand how the new developments affect their utility rates." Schwartzman notes TVA "has proposed nine new methane gas plants and three pipelines since 2020" plus advanced nuclear projects — [UT News, 2026-03-09](https://news.utk.edu/2026/03/09/schwartzman-part-of-team-awarded-1-million-sloan-foundation-grant/)

### Inferences
- **Common patterns of winners:**
  - Social-science lead or co-lead: anthropology, geography, public administration, sociology, economics or law. Engineers appear as co-investigators (Ilic at MIT on Verma's grant; Shafieezadeh on Dormady's) or inside engineering-and-public-policy groups (CMU).
  - Early-career leads (assistant professors such as Schwartzman, Ambrose, Osman, Luke, Kroot).
  - Comparative case-study designs across 2-5 states or sites, combined with one quantitative layer: cost analysis, demand-growth data, surveys, RCTs, or remote sensing in the Puerto Rico and ASU projects.
  - A practitioner partner or advisory board (SEPA, community organizations, Red Cross).
  - Outputs aimed at decision-makers.
  - Budgets: $250K for single early-career projects; $500K-$1M for multi-institution teams over about 3 years.
- **Why they were likely selected:** each is timely (the load-growth surge), names a specific institution type whose decisions matter (rural co-ops, PUCs, state siting regimes, RTOs, nuclear co-location), uses mixed methods and states practical outputs. This matches the "Timely, Catalytic, and Gap-Filling" and "Linking Research with Practice" criteria.
- **What is already covered:**
  - Tennessee ratepayer costs and TVA's response, qualitatively, with cost analyses (Emory/UTK).
  - State-level procedural governance (Stanford).
  - Western perceptions of clean-powered data centers (Utah State).
  - Nuclear co-location, including public responses (Michigan).
  - Rural co-op load growth (NC State).
  - Price-responsive commercial and industrial (C&I) electricity users (NBER).
- **What is not covered (opportunities):**
  - Counterfactual large-load tariff design and cost incidence inside a federal public-power wholesale system that serves 153 local power companies (LPCs). TVA adopted a new data center rate in August 2026; no funded project models alternative designs against it.
  - The terms under which data center load flexibility is credible and fairly priced. This links rate design with C&I use; NBER studies price response in markets, not TVA tariff design.
  - Local (county and municipal) rule dynamics (moratoria, zoning, preemption) as data over many jurisdictions, explaining what triggers them and how siting moves in response. Stanford is state-level; Emory has 3 state case studies.
  - Who is curtailed first in grid emergencies (data centers, industry or households) as load grows in a heat- and cold-stressed system. This touches resilience governance; Nguyen's VOLL equity grant is small and not about large loads.
  - Water and land conflicts and their valuation, with acceptance tied to measured local footprints.

## 2. Proposal ideas (led or co-led by Hao Lab with a social-science partner), screening and full evaluations

### Takeaway
Of 11 candidate ideas, the strongest LOI is **SL1, "Who pays for the AI load in public power?"** It models counterfactual large-load tariffs and their cost incidence across TVA's local power companies, built on Shi Jin's tariff- and moratorium-aware siting paper and co-led with an energy economist. It hits three of Sloan's five priorities (rate design, C&I use, affordability) and fills a gap the Emory/UTK grant leaves (tariff counterfactuals and quantitative incidence). **SL3, "Moving rules"** (an NLP-built panel of local data center rules across the seven TVA states, explaining moratoria and siting response) is the alternative, but it overlaps the CMMI idea C1 and needs a political-science or public-administration co-lead. SL9 (emergency curtailment and resilience governance) and SL4 (choice-experiment acceptance tied to measured footprints) are Revise. Coordinate any submission with the Emory/UTK team (Schwartzman, Luke) before sending, because a second Sloan data center grant at UT Knoxville on Tennessee ratepayers would look duplicative without a clear division of labor.

### Cited Findings

**Facts about the setting (used in the ideas)**
- TVA's Board (2026-08-20) "approved updates to TVA's wholesale rate structure to increase transparency, be more aligned to cost and protect residential and manufacturing customers from subsidizing the expenses associated with the significant growth of data center load"; "creates a new data center rate". TVA signed the "Ratepayer Protection Pledge". The 2026 IRP says the region "will need 11 to 32 gigawatts of additional generation capacity" by 2040. The FY2027 budget includes "4,120 megawatts of new TVA-owned capacity" — [TVA Board press release, 2026-08-20](https://s205.q4cdn.com/318912997/files/doc_news/2026/Aug/20/20260820-TVA-Board-Press-Release_FINAL.pdf)
- TVA sells power to the Knoxville Utilities Board, Huntsville Utilities and 151 other LPCs. News reports put the proposed data center rate at an average 10% increase from October. At the board meeting TVA did not say whether the rate covers all large customers or how stranded assets would be handled (search summary only; [WFAE](https://www.wfae.org/2026-08-25/tva-welcomes-data-centers-but-at-a-price); [NewsChannel 9](https://newschannel9.com/news/local/tva-proposes-new-data-center-power-rate-starting-in-october-with-10-average-increase-tennessee-valley-authority); [DCD](https://www.datacenterdynamics.com/en/news/us-utility-tva-imposes-new-rates-on-data-center-firms/))
- ThinkTennessee Part I: "Areas in Tennessee hosting data centers saw electricity bills rise 1.6%, on average, between 2023 and 2024, compared to the statewide average of 1.3%"; "Residential customers absorbed a 3.2% increase ... While commercial customers saw electricity bill reductions of 0.2%" — [ThinkTennessee, Powering the Boom Part I (July 2026)](https://www.thinktennessee.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/07/poweringtheboom-part1_report_thinktn_july2026.pdf)
- ThinkTennessee Part II: "Nearly 1,340 data centers are planned across [TVA's] seven-state territory". Tennessee has "new legislation prohibiting local governments and utilities from absorbing large (50MW+ capacity) data centers' infrastructure costs". "Nashville established the state's first local data center zoning restrictions, and officials across Tennessee are establishing temporary moratoriums". TVA's average system utilization was 53.1% (2016-2024). The TVA-Google partnership unlocked "approximately 1,000 MW of grid capacity across five local power companies by shifting non-urgent machine-learning workloads". The brief estimates 3,500-5,900 MW available through data center demand flexibility. During Winter Storm Elliott (December 2022) TVA "lost more than 6,000 MW of generation" — [ThinkTennessee, Powering the Boom Part II (Aug 2026)](https://www.thinktennessee.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/poweringtheboom-part2-policybrief_august2026.pdf)
- Google's TVA and Indiana Michigan Power agreements (August 2025) were described as the first demand-response deals targeting machine-learning workloads (search summary only; [Google Cloud blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/topics/sustainability/google-and-tva-data-center-flexibility); [APPA](https://www.publicpower.org/periodical/article/google-tva-enter-agreement-tied-data-center-demand-response))
- Tennessee household electricity burden: median 2.6% of income in 2024. About 17% of Tennessee households were highly or severely burdened — [Plank, UTC CRER White Paper 18 (Aug 2026)](https://www.utc.edu/sites/default/files/2026-08/wp-18-electricity-affordability-in-tennessee.pdf)

**Closest existing work (used for gap claims)**
- Harvard Electricity Law Initiative (Eliza Martin, Ari Peskoe, March 2025) reviewed nearly 50 utility commission proceedings on data center rates. It argues discounts shift costs to the public (legal and regulatory review, no TVA counterfactuals) (search summary; [Harvard ELI paper](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/extracting-profits-from-the-public-how-utility-ratepayers-are-paying-for-big-tech/))
- LBNL "Electricity Rate Designs for Large Loads: Evolving Practices and Opportunities, 2026 Update" covers how utilities and regulators manage the risks of insufficient supply or underutilized investment. Per the search summary it reviews a sample of 55 tariffs (descriptive) — [LBNL ETA](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/publications/electricity-rate-designs-large-0)
- Scalera, Bosetti and Pecci, "Large-Load Electricity Customers and Spillovers on Household Prices: Evidence from U.S. Data Centers," CEPR DP21854 (2026-08). A shift-share design finds household price effects and says residential increases are "close to zero in territories served by nonprofit utilities" (search summary only; the CEPR page was blocked by Cloudflare; [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cpr:ceprdp:21854); [VoxEU](https://cepr.org/voxeu/columns/how-data-centres-affect-electricity-prices-and-whom))
- Zhou et al., arXiv 2611.16095: 371 announced Texas projects (38.9 GW) raise modeled residential costs by 1.64 cents/kWh in one case, 68% via wholesale prices and 32% via transmission charges. Excluding data centers from the transmission cost-sharing base raises it to 1.87 cents/kWh (ERCOT market setting, not public power) — [arXiv 2611.16095](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16095)
- E3 (consultancy) found "no evidence of a historical or present-day cost shift" in the largest market and counted at least 38 large-load tariffs since 2018 (search summary only; [E3 May 2026](https://www.ethree.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Understanding-the-Drivers-of-Rising-Electricity-Rates-and-the-Role-of-Data-Centers_E3-2026.pdf)). A Rutgers analysis of 2,277 data centers calls the evidence "weak, mixed, and small in magnitude" (search summary only; [Rutgers Policy Lab](https://policylab.rutgers.edu/?p=11153)). The empirical literature on spillovers is therefore contested.
- Vanderbilt Policy Accelerator, "Nine Ways to Address the Energy Impacts of AI Data Centers" (Hannah Wiseman and Matthew McHale, January 2026). Its first section is "Protecting Households, Consumers, and Communities from Rate Increases" (policy recommendations, not modeling) — [VPA report](https://cdn.vanderbilt.edu/vu-URL/wp-content/uploads/sites/412/2026/01/12211201/Nine-Ways-to-Address-the-Energy-Impacts-of-AI-Data-Centers.pdf)
- Local moratorium trackers disagree because definitions differ. National League of Cities: at least 76 local governments attempted moratoriums or bans. GatherGov: 632 jurisdictions took formal action since January 2026. Georgia Tech EPIC: almost 40 Georgia local governments. Bipartisan Policy Center: 54 local moratoria (all search summaries; [Route Fifty](https://www.route-fifty.com/artificial-intelligence/2026/08/many-localities-have-tried-data-center-moratoriums-tracker-finds/415696/); [E&E News](https://www.eenews.net/articles/coming-to-a-county-near-you-a-data-center-moratorium/); [Columbia Climate Law blog](https://blogs.law.columbia.edu/climatechange/2026/05/27/local-moratoria-considerations/)). No tracker found explains why moratoria are adopted or links them to siting outcomes.
- Acceptance surveys: UVA National Security Institute Data Center Opinion Project (3,200 Americans, June 2026: more than 3 in 5 oppose a local data center); Pew 2026 polls; Utah State statewide survey; a Korean discrete-choice study of willingness to accept (EGU26-4629). No U.S. choice experiment tied to measured local footprints was found (search summaries; [UVA](https://nationalsecurity.virginia.edu/research/data-center-opinion-project); [EGU26-4629](https://meetingorganizer.copernicus.org/EGU26/EGU26-4629.html); [USU Today](https://www.usu.edu/today/story/utah-survey-uncovers-why-residents-oppose-new-data-centers))
- Tracked lab ideas with data center content: CMMI C1 (siting under endogenous rules, with a hazard model fitted to moratorium data), CBET E1, CAREER K1, NOAA N1 (heat-driven cascades in the Tennessee Valley energy-water system), USGS 104g U1/U2/U4, USGS 104(b) FY2027 I3/I5 — `funding/summaries/2026-10.md` sections 3-4 and `funding/2026-11-19.md` Ideas Overview (repository files)

#### Full evaluation: SL1 — Who pays for the AI load in public power? Large-load tariff design and cost incidence across TVA's local power companies

- **Call:** Sloan E&E, unsolicited LOI (rolling).
- **Team:** Yuefeng Hao (co-lead; Assistant Professor, ISE), Shi Jin (PhD student; tariff- and moratorium-aware siting model), Yifan Gao (PhD student; interconnection-queue uncertainty, workload scheduling for the flexibility aim). Co-lead needed: an **energy economist with rate-design and public-power expertise** (candidate: Charles B. Sims, UT Baker School and Economics; see collaborator notes). Optional: Mingzhou Jin (registry; optimization). Advisory board: LPC economists (for example EPB Chattanooga's community economist), TVA staff as observers, a nonpartisan state think tank. No one has been contacted.
- **Research question:**
  1. Under TVA's August 2026 data center rate and Tennessee's 50 MW+ cost law, how are the costs of 11-32 GW of new capacity shared among data centers, manufacturers, small businesses and households across LPCs?
  2. Which alternative tariff designs keep residential bills and energy burden flat while still serving large loads?
  3. How do those designs change where data centers locate?
- **Approach:**
  - **Aim 1 (rates):** build a transparent cost-allocation model of TVA's wholesale rate and LPC retail rates from public TVA, EIA and LPC data, and run it under IRP load scenarios.
  - **Aim 2 (design):** compare TVA's rate with counterfactuals (minimum-take/take-or-pay, exit fees and collateral, cost-based demand charges, flexibility credits tied to verified workload shifting), with incidence by customer class and LPC.
  - **Aim 3 (behavior):** feed each tariff into Jin's siting model to show the siting response and its feedback on cost sharing, and interview LPC and state stakeholders to test which designs are administrable.
  - **Distributional layer:** household energy burden by LPC, from SL6.
- **Closest existing projects and papers, and the gap:**
  - Emory/UTK ($1M) studies ratepayer costs qualitatively and with cost analyses across GA, VA and TN, plus maps and regulatory comparison ([Emory News](https://news.emory.edu/stories/2026/01/esc_sloan_grant_funds_15-01-2026/story.html)). It does not appear to model alternative tariff designs or siting response (inference from public descriptions).
  - Harvard ELI is a legal review of about 50 proceedings ([ELI](https://eelp.law.harvard.edu/extracting-profits-from-the-public-how-utility-ratepayers-are-paying-for-big-tech/)); LBNL is a descriptive tariff survey ([LBNL](https://eta-publications.lbl.gov/publications/electricity-rate-designs-large-0)).
  - Zhou et al. model an ERCOT market, not a public-power cost-of-service system ([arXiv 2611.16095](https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.16095)). Scalera et al. estimate national spillovers and report near-zero residential effects under nonprofit utilities ([EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cpr:ceprdp:21854)). TVA's LPCs are a direct test of that mechanism under new rules.
  - The NBER grant to Ito, Johnston and Reguant studies price-responsive C&I users in markets (Sloan grants database), not tariff design in public power.
  - Stanford (Osman) compares state procedural norms, not tariffs (Sloan grant purpose).
- **Novelty:** the first counterfactual analysis, as far as these searches show, of large-load tariff designs in TVA's federal wholesale and local-retail public-power structure. It links cost incidence by LPC and customer class with endogenous siting response.
- **Why us (talent pool facts only):**
  - Shi Jin's ongoing paper "Siting Under Moving Rules: A Tariff- and Moratorium-Aware Optimization Model for AI Data Center Location in the Tennessee Valley" (with Y. Hao).
  - Yifan Gao's ongoing paper on robust siting under interconnection-queue uncertainty, and Gao's stated interest in geo-distributed AI workload scheduling (for flexibility credits).
  - The PI's funded UT ISSE grant on the local footprint of AI data centers (noise, water, land-market tradeoffs in Tennessee) and funded USGS 104(b) grant on decision support for TDEC on data center and SMR withdrawals.
  - The submitted paper "Regional screening of electricity-related water consumption for projected data-center load growth" (Kaylee Jin and Y. Hao).
  - The PI's co-authorship of "Wildfire–Power Grid Interactions" (RSER 2026) with Fangxing Fran Li and Mingzhou Jin.
  - The PI's teaching of IE 591 AI for Engineer Managers and IE 200 Engineering Statistics (training of students).
- **Fundability against Sloan's priorities:**
  - It directly addresses "electricity rate design", "commercial and industrial use" and "energy affordability", and adds "governance" through LPC and TVA decision processes.
  - It is US-focused, non-advocacy (evaluates designs, takes no position), timely (rate adopted August 2026) and gap-filling.
  - It trains two PhD students, links research with practice through the LPC advisory board, and suits early-career leadership (PI is an Assistant Professor since 08/2026).
  - Suggested size: about $250K over 2 years (approved year-round), or $400-500K over 3 years if the economist's group and a survey are added (quarterly approval).
- **Risks:**
  - The social-science core must be real: without an economist co-lead it reads as engineering. Mitigation: an economist co-lead and an economics PhD student or postdoc line.
  - Overlap with Emory/UTK at the same university and on the same state. Mitigation: talk to Schwartzman and Luke first; propose a quantitative complement (tariff counterfactuals, not ethnography or community mapping) and ask whether they would serve on the advisory board.
  - Data access: LPC-level rate and load data are public only in part.
  - The PI has no prior economics publications.
  - TVA politics: stay strictly non-advocacy.
  - The empirical literature on spillovers is contested (E3 and Rutgers vs Scalera et al.), so frame the work as design analysis, not a claim that costs are already shifting.
- **Verdict: Pursue** as the single Sloan LOI. Conditions: an economist co-lead, a talk with the Emory/UTK team, and SL2 (flexibility credits) and SL6 (energy burden) folded in as aims.

#### Full evaluation: SL3 — Moving rules: local data center governance across the TVA region and how siting responds

- **Team:** Yuefeng Hao (co-lead), Yifan Gao (NLP and information extraction; source-linked requirements dataset), Shi Jin (data pipelines from the 350K-paper ETL platform; siting model). Co-lead needed: a **political scientist or public administration scholar** on local or collaborative governance (candidates: Graham Ambrose, NC State; Joshua A. Basseches, Case Western Reserve).
- **Research question:** Why do counties and cities in the seven TVA states adopt moratoria, zoning restrictions, overlay districts or benefit agreements for data centers? Is it water, noise, rates, land, partisanship or state preemption? How does the resulting patchwork redirect development and its costs?
- **Approach:** build a source-linked panel of local actions from minutes, ordinances and news (NLP extraction with human validation and an error audit); estimate event-history and diffusion models of adoption; interview local officials in matched cases; feed rule scenarios into the siting model to measure relocation.
- **Closest existing work and gap:** Stanford's Sloan grant compares states, not localities (grant purpose). The NLC, GatherGov, Electric Choice and BPC trackers count actions but disagree and do not explain them ([Route Fifty](https://www.route-fifty.com/artificial-intelligence/2026/08/many-localities-have-tried-data-center-moratoriums-tracker-finds/415696/)). Oregon State's Sloan grant studies siting outcomes for battery storage, not data centers ([ESI page](https://sloan.org/programs/research/energy-and-environment/announcing-energy-system-interactions-rfp-grantees)). Emory/UTK compares three states' regulatory landscapes.
- **Novelty:** a validated local-rule panel across one wholesale power system, so state and local rules vary while wholesale supply is shared, linked to a siting response model.
- **Why us:** Gao's NLP background and example project "Construct a source-linked dataset of data center development requirements and assess how information extraction errors affect downstream siting decisions"; Jin's moratorium-aware siting paper; Jin's 350K-scale ETL and retrieval pipeline.
- **Fundability:** addresses "energy system resilience and governance" and "Place-based Energy Transitions". Social-science core: political science or public administration.
- **Risks:**
  - Overlap with CMMI C1 (`funding/summaries/2026-10.md`), which plans "an empirical hazard model fitted to moratorium and opposition data". Two funders paying for one dataset is a duplicate-funding risk. Mitigation: the Sloan proposal owns the governance dataset and its explanation; C1 uses it only as an input and lists Sloan as pending support.
  - Data quality of local records.
  - Only partly answers the priorities on rates and affordability.
- **Verdict: Pursue as the alternative** if SL1 cannot secure an economist co-lead, or as a second LOI only after SL1 is answered. Do not send both at once.
